# How to find a story

Five steps from a stray image to something the template can hold.
`TEMPLATE.md` is the shape; this is how you get something worth pouring into it.

---

## Step 1 — The image

A cool, descriptive thing that happened somewhere in the world. Concrete and seen: a
place, a moment, weather, an object. Not a plot — a photograph.

> In Huldugaard, the plague passed them by. They stood on the hill and watched the smoke
> of other people's pyres for a month, and not one of their own took sick.

**Ready when:** you can see it. If it's a summary rather than a picture, keep going.

## Step 2 — The moral

What does it say about people, or about how the world works? One line, plain, and it
should feel slightly uncomfortable to write down.

> Being spared is not the same as being innocent — and nobody who watched you survive
> will ever believe the difference.

**Ready when:** you'd be reluctant to say it out loud at a table. Comfortable morals make
flat stories.
**Rule:** this line must never appear in the story. It's the thing the reader concludes,
not the thing you tell them.

## Step 3 — The person, and the choice

Steps 1 and 2 don't move. An image sits still and a moral is already true. What turns
them into a story is **someone with something to lose, making a decision you can judge
them for.**

Three things, in order:

1. **Whose eyes.** Not the most important person in the image — the one with the most to
   lose in it.
2. **What they want.** This must be *the opposite of the moral.* If your moral says a lie
   can be a kindness, your character wants to be honest. If the moral says being spared
   looks like guilt, your character wants to be seen as good. The moral has to cost them
   something to learn, or the story agrees with itself and there's nothing to watch.
3. **What they choose.** One sentence. The thing they'd rather nobody found out.

> A Huldu healer who knows exactly why her people were spared. She wants to be trusted by
> the villages below — she has spent her life earning it. She could save them by telling
> them what she knows. Telling them also reveals what her people are.
> **She keeps it to herself.**

**Ready when:** the choice isn't "they decide to go." Going is Act 2. And when a decent
person could plausibly choose either way — if the right answer is obvious, you've written
a test, not a story.

## Step 4 — The price

What the choice costs, made small and specific. Not "many died" — one named person, one
concrete loss, one face.

> One winter later she watches a particular child die of it. The mother thanks her for
> trying.

**Ready when:** it's a single image again, like step 1. Abstract prices don't land.

## Step 5 — Into the acts

Now the template fills itself:

| Step | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| 1 — the image | Act 1, the world as a pressure |
| 3 — what they want | Act 1, the misfit and the lure |
| 3 — the choice | Act 3, the immediate consequence |
| what the choice buys | Act 4, the long-term consequence |
| 4 — the price | Act 5, the final consequence |
| 2 — the moral | nowhere. It's what's left in the reader. |

Then run the chain test from the spark sheet: cross out the choice — do Act 4 and Act 5
still happen anyway? If yes, go back to step 3.

---

## The short version

1. Something you can see.
2. Something true you'd rather not say.
3. Someone who wants the opposite of it, and does something about it.
4. What it costs them, with a face on it.
5. Pour into the five acts.
