# The Light

## Act 1 — The Ordinary World · 16–21 lines

High above the sea, the gnomes of Nisaland lived in tunnels carve into cliffs.

Life here was a constant battle for survival. Each winter crept in colder and crueler than the one before, bringing howling winds that lashed the cliffs. The sea below was wild and untamed.

Among them lived a young gnome named Brimble, who felt out of place in this unforgiving world. He was restless, unwilling to accept the endless cycle of cold and struggle. Every night, he would gaze out of the hole in the mountain, staring out at the horizon. His breath clouded the air, curling like steam from a tiny machine.

One night, a shimmering light caught his attention in the horizon. It was too steady to be a star and too bright to be moonlight glinting off the ice. Curious, he asked the elders about it. "It's just the ice playing tricks," they said dismissively. Others called it a distant reflection, nothing more. But Brimble couldn't let it go.

To him, the light wasn't just a mystery, it was a calling. Night after night, he stood watching, unable to tear his gaze away. The cold stung his face and numbed his fingers, but the glow seemed to pulse like something alive, waiting for him.

"I'll go to it," he told the others one evening. "No matter the cost."

The gnomes shook their heads and whispered behind his back. "Poor Brimble," they muttered. "He's losing his grip."

Eventually they talked him out of it, seeing the impossibility crossing the rough sea.

## Act 2 — The Inciting Incident · 14–20 lines

But one day the ocean froze, Brimble seized the chance to cross it, ignoring the warnings of his kin.

The journey was brutal. Cold winds cut through him, and loneliness gnawed at his resolve. At last, he reached the light, only to find it was a polished mountain wall reflecting the sun. Crushed by his folly, he believed he'd perish there.

## Act 3 — The Immediate Consequence · 14–20 lines

> **[OUTLINE]**
> He meets the humans and they treat him well. They respect his skill for craftsmanship,
> which was common in his culture. He meets King Uther of Norinheim and solves a complex
> mathematical or scientific problem for him. Uther offers him money and food enough to
> bring his people over. He accepts.

*Needed for the chain:* the choice he'll be judged for. Right now Uther makes the offer
and Berrick simply accepts — no cost, no lie, nothing to answer for later. **This is where
the lie belongs:** he goes home and tells them an angel guided him to a promised land,
rather than admit he chased a reflection and was rescued by humans.

## Act 4 — The Long-Term Consequence · 14–20 lines

> **[OUTLINE]**
> He brings the gnomes to the mountain, where he is their leader. The population grows.
> He is happy, and sees the prospects of his people.

*Needed:* the first crack at the end — the old Nisaland elders arriving with their own
authority, or the first gnome who asks a question he can't answer honestly.

## Act 5 — The Final Consequence · 14–20 lines

Two endings are on the table. They can't both be it.

**A. The lie.** A young gnome asks for the truth and he confesses. Word spreads; his
people feel betrayed and hang their king. As the noose tightens he finds peace — they
thrived in the warmth of the new home, and for that, the lie was worth it.

**B. The coup.** The alliance with Uther dies out, and the political elite from the old
mountain take his leadership by force.

**A is the one that fits the template.** Apply the Act 5 check — *could this ending happen
if he had never acted?* Ending B could: the alliance lapses and rivals move, none of it
caused by anything Berrick did. Ending A is caused entirely by his own choice in Act 3,
and it closes on the meaning rather than on politics. B works as the first crack at the
end of Act 4, or as the pressure that makes the confession dangerous — not as the ending.
